# The Big Three Trap: Why Open Weights Are Squeezing Closed AI Labs

The competitive landscape in AI is changing faster than many expected.

Just a year ago, proprietary models held a clear lead in capability, making premium API pricing relatively easy to justify. Today, that gap is narrowing. Recent open-weight models such as Kimi K3, along with the latest Gemma and other large open models, deliver performance that is increasingly competitive for coding, reasoning, and agentic workflows while running at a fraction of the cost.

This isn't simply another benchmark cycle. It represents a structural shift in the economics of AI.

Open models are pushing prices down from below. Hyperscalers are willing to commoditize inference from above. And enterprise customers—the source of the highest-value interactions—are increasingly unwilling to contribute their data back into proprietary training pipelines.

Closed AI labs are being squeezed from every direction.

## When History Rhymes

History doesn't repeat itself, but it often rhymes.

As technological advantages narrow, incumbents often look for other ways to preserve their position. Regulation is one of the oldest tools available.

The U.S. auto industry offers a useful parallel. By the late 1970s, Japanese manufacturers had built vehicles that challenged Detroit on price, quality, and manufacturing efficiency. The response wasn't limited to improving factories. It also included lobbying for policies such as Voluntary Export Restraints that slowed foreign competition while domestic firms adapted.

AI is obviously a different industry, but the incentives feel familiar.

Over the past several years, frontier AI companies have increasingly supported proposals for compute thresholds, licensing regimes, and centralized deployment requirements. Many of these proposals are motivated by legitimate safety concerns. They also have an economic consequence: they raise the cost of participation for smaller competitors while favoring organizations with the capital and legal resources to navigate complex regulation.

Whether by design or as a byproduct, regulation becomes another competitive moat as technical moats begin to shrink.

## Google's Different Bet

While many frontier labs depend on premium API margins, Google is playing a different game.

Google doesn't need inference to be a high-margin business. Its incentives span Cloud, Workspace, Search, Android, and developer infrastructure. Cheap inference strengthens the broader ecosystem, even if individual model margins decline.

That explains why Google has aggressively invested in fast, inexpensive models like Gemini Flash while continuing to release capable open-weight models through Gemma.

For most production workloads, developers care less about winning another benchmark than they do about latency, reliability, and cost. Processing documents, summarizing logs, routing support tickets, or coordinating background agents rarely requires the most powerful model available. It requires one that is fast, inexpensive, and good enough.

For companies with broader platform businesses, commoditizing inference is a feature, not a threat.

Pure API vendors don't have that luxury.

## The Data Flywheel Is Breaking

Conventional wisdom says the company with the most users wins because it collects the most data, trains the best models, and attracts even more users.

That flywheel is beginning to break.

The highest-value AI interactions are no longer casual chatbot conversations. They happen inside software engineering teams, enterprise workflows, legal reviews, financial analysis, and internal business processes.

Those are precisely the environments where customers demand Zero Data Retention, strict contractual privacy guarantees, and complete control over their information.

The irony is hard to ignore.

The most valuable training data increasingly exists behind contracts that prohibit it from being used for training.

Closed labs must therefore rely more heavily on synthetic data generation, human evaluation, and expensive post-training pipelines rather than continuously learning from real-world enterprise usage.

Meanwhile, open-weight ecosystems benefit from a rapidly expanding community of researchers, synthetic data pipelines, and organizations improving models in public.

The traditional proprietary data advantage is becoming far less durable than many expected.

## Lock-In Is Also Weakening

Enterprise customers have learned an expensive lesson from previous generations of software: avoid vendor lock-in whenever possible.

Modern AI infrastructure reflects that lesson.

Applications increasingly sit behind abstraction layers that allow developers to swap models with minimal effort. If an application targets a model-agnostic interface, replacing one provider with another may require little more than changing a configuration value.

As open models continue improving, switching costs continue falling.

That makes it increasingly difficult to sustain premium pricing based solely on proprietary model access.

## What Comes Next

If inference becomes a commodity, then selling tokens alone is unlikely to remain a durable business model.

The companies that succeed will probably own something more valuable than the model itself.

They will own developer ecosystems, open protocols, orchestration frameworks, enterprise infrastructure, and the tools that coordinate increasingly heterogeneous fleets of models.

The most capable frontier models won't disappear. They'll become part of a larger system—handling the hardest reasoning tasks while cheaper local or open-weight models execute the routine work.

The first decade of modern AI rewarded whoever built the best model.

The next decade may reward whoever builds the best platform.

That's a very different competition.
